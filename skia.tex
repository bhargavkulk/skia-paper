\section{Skia}

We define 2 languages in this section, \surface{} and
\core.
\surface{} corresponds to the \texttt{SkCanvas} API a user of Skia
typically uses to draw pictures.

\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{S M c T}
    \surface{} & s & ::= & $c$ \\
    \addlinespace[1pt]
    Command & c & ::= & Draw($g$, $p$) \\
               &   & | & DrawText(string, $p$) \\
               &   & | & Clip($cl$, Intersect | Difference) \\
               &   & | & Save(); $c$; Restore() \\
               &   & | & SaveLayer($p$); $c$; Restore() \\
               &   & | & $c$; $c$ \\
    \addlinespace[1pt]
    Geometry & g,\;cl & ::= & Rect($\ldots$) | RRect($\ldots$) | Path($\ldots$) \\
    \addlinespace[1pt]
    Paint & p & ::= & Paint($f$, $st$, $c_f$, $b$) \\
    \addlinespace[1pt]
    Fill & f & ::= & Color(pixel) | LinearGradient($\ldots$) | $\ldots$ \\
    \addlinespace[1pt]
    Style & st & ::= & Solid | Stroke \\
    \addlinespace[1pt]
    Filter & f & ::= & Id | Luma | $\ldots$ \\
    \addlinespace[1pt]
    BlendMode & b & ::= & SrcOver | DstIn | Src | $\ldots$\\
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}

A drawing described by the \texttt{SkCanvas} API,
  and by extension a \surface{} program,
  is managed by a canvas.
A canvas maintains 2 types of state:
  a layer state and a clip state.
The layer state describes the pixel buffer currently managed by the program.
It can also be described as the current surface that is being drawn into.
While a layer is conceptually infinite,
  only the part of the layer that is within the set bounds of the canvas.
  is ``drawn''.
Consider the following \surface{} program,
  which draws into a canvas of size 256$\times$256.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Draw(Rect(0, 0, 192, 192), DEFAULT_PAINT)
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing1.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

The layer state before the \texttt{Draw} command
  is an empty pixel buffer.
The layer state after the \texttt{Draw} command
  is a pixel buffer with a black square.
It's easy to see how a \texttt{Draw} command
  manipulates the layer state
  by the drawing a specified geometry
  onto the layer.
But just ``drawing'' geometries on a layer
  is a pretty bad drawing API
  as we have no way to describe
  how the pixels within the geometry are filled.
That role is filled by \texttt{Paint}s.

\begin{equation*}
\texttt{Paint($f$, $st$, $c_f$, $b$)}
\end{equation*}

A \texttt{Paint} in \surface{} is made of 4 components:
  fill, style, color filter and blend mode.
Each of these components change how a geometry is drawn.
The fill defines the mapping from geometry to pixel color values in the buffer.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Draw(Rect(0, 0, 192, 192),
      Paint(Color((1, 1, 0, 0)), ...))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing2.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

A fill does not need to be a constant fill of the same pixel.
More interesting fills can be implemented with gradients:

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Draw(Rect(0, 0, 192, 192),
      Paint(LinearGradient(...), ...))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing3.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

A style modifies the geometry that is being drawn.
The \texttt{Solid} style,
  the default style,
  fills the entire geometry.
While a \texttt{Stroke} style
  only fills the boundaries of the geometry.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Draw(Rect(48, 96, 112, 160),
      Paint(..., Fill, ...));
Draw(Rect(114, 96, 208, 160),
      Paint(..., Stroke, ...))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing4.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

A color filter further modifies the pixels filled in by \texttt{Fill}.
It can be a considered as a function from pixels to pixels.
The default color filter is basically a function that returns pixels unmodified.
The following example applies a ``luminance filter'' over the earlier gradient example.
It turns brightness,
  as percieved by the human eyes,
  into transparency.
Brighter pixels are more opaque,
  and darker pixels more transparent.\footnote{Red is brighter than blue if we
    consider their relative luminances.}

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Draw(Rect(0, 0, 192, 192),
     Paint(..., LumaFilter, ...))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing5.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

Finally, a blend mode describes how
  the geometry is drawn on to existing pixels of the current layer state.
A blend mode is essentially a binary operator over pixels.
The default blend mode is \texttt{SrcOver},
  and it describes what is commonly known as ``alpha compositing''.
We may even write down \texttt{SrcOver} as an algebraic equation over pixels.

\begin{equation*}
\texttt{SrcOver}(d,\; s) = s + (1-s.\texttt{alpha})\times d
\tag*{\text{\footnotemark}}
\end{equation*}
\footnotetext{Instead of describing the blend mode separately over each channel
of the pixel, we instead describe it over all channels simultaneously.}

We follow the usual terminology used in describing blend modes:
  $s$ or \textit{source} are the pixels being drawn
  while $d$ or \textit{destination} are the pixels being draw on.
Under \texttt{SrcOver}, the source pixel is placed over the destination pixel
  with the destination contribution reduced according to the source's opacity.
When the source pixels are opaque,
  the results is effectively like drawing the source pixels ``over'' the
  destination pixels.
In the following example,
  we blend one square over another using \texttt{SrcOver} blending.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Draw(Rect(0, 0, 192, 192),
      Paint(Color((1, 1, 0, 0)), ..., SrcOver));
Draw(Rect(64, 64, 256, 256),
      Paint(Color((1, 0, 0, 1)), ..., SrcOver))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing6.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

Another blend mode commonly used is \texttt{DstIn}.

\begin{equation*}
\texttt{DstIn}(d,\; s) = d \times s.\texttt{alpha}
\end{equation*}

If the fill of the source geometry is opaque,
  the source acts like a ``mask''.
The pixels of dst that
  are not under the opaque pixels of source
  are masked out.
The following program is the \texttt{SrcOver} example,
  but the second square has its blend mode changed to \texttt{DstIn}.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Draw(Rect(0, 0, 192, 192),
      Paint(Color((1, 1, 0, 0)), ..., SrcOver));
Draw(Rect(64, 64, 256, 256),
      Paint(Color((1, 0, 0, 1)), ..., DstIn))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing8.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

While \texttt{DstIn} can be used to mask destination pixels in this way,
  \surface{} provides a dedicated clipping command for expressing this
  operation more directly.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Clip(Rect(64, 64, 256, 256), Intersect);
Draw(Rect(0, 0, 192, 192),
      Paint(Color((1, 1, 0, 0)), ..., SrcOver))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing9.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

Now instead masking being a consequence of blending,
  we mask by constraining the draw geometry
  by intersecting it with a clip geometry.
\texttt{Clip} commands modify the clip state for the current layer;
  A clip will affect all consequent \texttt{Draw} commands,
  as you can see with the following example.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Clip(Rect(64, 64, 192, 192), Intersect);
Draw(Rect(0, 0, 128, 128),
      Paint(Color((1, 1, 0, 0)), ..., SrcOver));
Draw(Rect(128, 128, 256, 256),
      Paint(Color((1, 0, 0, 1)), ..., SrcOver))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing10.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

If we only use \texttt{Clip} and \texttt{Draw} commands,
  there is no way to reset the global clip state.
In \surface{} this is accomplished with the compound
  \texttt{Save(); ...; Restore()} commands
  and by using a \textit{global clip stack} instead of a singular global clip
  state.
A \texttt{Save()} duplicates the current top clip state on the clip stack.
All subsequent \texttt{Clip} commands will modify the new top clip state.
A \texttt{Restore()} will then pop the clip state duplicated by \texttt{Save},
  discarding all changes made to the that clip state.
To understand this better, lets surround both the \texttt{Clip} and the first
\texttt{Draw} command of the previous example with \texttt{Save(); ...;
  Restore()}.
The \texttt{Clip} will now only affects the first square and not the second one.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Save();
  Clip(Rect(64, 64, 192, 192), Intersect);
  Draw(Rect(0, 0, 128, 128),
        Paint(Color((1, 1, 0, 0)), ..., SrcOver));
Restore();
Draw(Rect(128, 128, 256, 256),
      Paint(Color((1, 0, 0, 1)), ..., SrcOver))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing11.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}


It is useful to understand \texttt{Save(); ...; Restore()} commands
 as a grouping operation;
 the effects of \texttt{Clip}s within a \texttt{Save(); ...; Restore()} group
 are limited to only to the \texttt{Draw}s within the group.
But with the current set of commands, we do not have a way to ``group''
  the consequences of layer state modifying commands.
Consider the \fillin{\surface{} program 6}.
What if we want to make the whole drawing translucent.
As a first try we could decrease the opacity of each square.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
Draw(Rect(0, 0, 192, 192),
      Paint(Color((0.5, 1, 0, 0)), ..., SrcOver));
Draw(Rect(64, 64, 256, 256),
      Paint(Color((0.5, 0, 0, 1)), ..., SrcOver))
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing12.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

This is not what we intended to draw with this program.
\texttt{SaveLayer($p$); ...; Restore()} commands help us group drawing effects
  in a useful way.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{BVerbatim}
SaveLayer(Paint(Color(0.5, 0, 0, 0)), ..., SrcOver);
  Draw(Rect(0, 0, 192, 192),
        Paint(Color((1, 1, 0, 0)), ..., SrcOver));
  Draw(Rect(64, 64, 256, 256),
        Paint(Color((1, 0, 0, 1)), ..., SrcOver));
Restore()
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.45\textwidth}
\centering
\frame{\includegraphics[scale=0.3]{drawing13.pdf}}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

This is accomplised by extending the global layer state to a stack.
All \texttt{Draw} operations occur on the current top layer state.
A \texttt{SaveLayer},
  like \texttt{Save},
  duplicates the current clip on the stack.
It also pushes a new layer on the layer stack.
All operations after a \texttt{SaveLayer}
  now modify the new top layer on the stack.
Just like \texttt{Save},
  a matching \texttt{Restore} will pop the clip state stack.
It will also pop the the top layer of the layer stack,
  and then ``draw'' it on layer below it.
Just like \texttt{Draw},
  \texttt{Paint} controls how the top layer
  is combined with the bottom layer.
Like in the example above,
  \texttt{Fill} controls the overall opacity of the layer before combining.
It is important to note that only the alpha channel of the fill is used,
  all other channels are useless to \texttt{SaveLayer}.
\footnote{If the \texttt{Fill} is a gradient, the alpha is assumed to be 1}
\texttt{Style} is not used because \texttt{SaveLayer} has no geometry to draw.
A \texttt{Filter} is applied to the entire Layer just like in \texttt{Draw}.
Finally,
  \texttt{BlendMode}s describe how the top layer
  is combined on the bottom layer.
